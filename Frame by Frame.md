# Storyline

Every animated film starts as a few sentences. This guide follows one small example, a round blue character named Pip who wants to reach a lamp on a high shelf, from idea to finished motion.

## Logline

A small round character cannot reach a glowing lamp. After a few failed hops, it learns that the big jump needs a crouch first.

## Three acts, counted in frames

ACT I · 0–6 s · frames 0–144

### Setup

Pip sits under a shelf and notices the lamp. The lamp is the goal, and it is clearly too high.

ACT II · 6–22 s · frames 144–528

### Confrontation

Pip tries three hops and each one falls short. Pip notices that the crouch before a hop decides how high it goes.

ACT III · 22–30 s · frames 528–720

### Resolution

One long crouch, one big jump. Pip touches the lamp and the room lights up.

## Tension over time

A beat sheet is easier to judge as a curve. The peak should land late, and the ending should drop quickly.

## Keyframes

### What to look for

The crouch before the jump squashes Pip wide and short. The launch stretches Pip tall. These exaggerations are called squash and stretch, and they make the weight of a character readable.

At 8 fps the motion steps visibly. At 24 fps it is the film standard. At 30 fps it matches common video.

## Frames per second

FPS

Hand-drawn animation often uses 12 fps, with each drawing held for two film frames. Film uses 24. Each frame shows for 83 ms at this rate.
